# Gym — lower body, eccentric

> DRAFT — coach to verify. General strength-and-conditioning guidance, not
> individualised medical advice.

## Use when

Emphasising the **lowering** phase to drive hypertrophy, fascicle-length
adaptation and eccentric-specific strength. The clearest applications:

- Adding eccentric hamstring capacity in an athlete with a hamstring-strain
  history, or one doing repeated high-speed running.
- Breaking a hypertrophy plateau where concentric-focused work has stalled.
- Building tolerance to the braking demands of downhill running, deceleration
  and change of direction.

## Don't use when

- The athlete is inside 10 days of an event, or in a deload — this is the first
  session to remove.
- A tendon is currently reactive and painful. Eccentric-only loading is **not**
  the best answer for a painful tendon (see Evidence notes); use
  `gym/lower-isometric` or heavy slow resistance from `gym/lower-max-strength`.
- It's the athlete's first week of structured lifting, or their first week back
  after time off. The soreness response to unaccustomed eccentric work is
  disproportionate and will cost the following week.
- The week already contains a hard running day within 72 h that cannot move.

## Prescription

Pick **one** variant. Do not combine tempo and supramaximal work in one session.

| Variant | Sets × reps | Load / tempo | Rest | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tempo eccentric | 3–4 × 4–8 | moderate–heavy, 3–5 s lowering | 2–3 min | Default. Squat, RDL, split squat, leg press |
| Supramaximal eccentric | 3–4 × 2–4 | >100% concentric 1RM, lowering only | 3–4 min | Advanced. Needs a spotter or rig; assisted on the way up |
| Nordic hamstring curl | 2–3 × 4–6 | bodyweight, control as far as possible | 2 min | Start at the very bottom of this range |
| Eccentric calf / heel drop | 3 × 8–12 | bodyweight → loaded | 90 s | Achilles-specific; slow, controlled lowering |

**Session shape:** one primary eccentric lift, then 1–2 non-eccentric
accessories if volume allows. Total eccentric working sets should stay ≤8 for
the first month of a block.

## Dosing and progression

- **Progress the tempo before the load.** Moving 3 s → 5 s lowering at the same
  weight is a real progression and is easier to recover from than adding plates.
- **Ramp volume conservatively** — add roughly one set per week, not one
  exercise. The soreness response to a jump in eccentric volume is the largest
  of any session type here.
- **Nordics specifically:** higher-volume protocols produce larger gains in
  eccentric strength, fascicle length and muscle thickness than low-volume ones,
  so there is a real reason to build toward volume — but the first two weeks
  should still be near-trivial. Two sets of three, hurting nobody, beats one
  ambitious session that costs the athlete the next five days.
- **Regression path:** shorten the tempo → reduce range → reduce sets → swap to
  `gym/lower-isometric`.

## Scheduling

**Hard rules** (breaking these makes the plan wrong):
- ≥72 h before running intervals, intensive plyometrics or a power session.
- Never in a taper, race week, or the 10 days before a key event.
- Not on the same day as any session in `same_day_conflicts`.

**Preferences** (trade these away when the calendar forces it):
- Early in the microcycle — Monday or Tuesday in a Saturday-event week.
- After a rest or easy day, not after the long session.
- Pair the following day with upper body, isometrics, or easy Z2 cycling.
  Cycling tolerates sore legs far better than running does.

**What it costs the next 72 h:** peak DOMS at 24–72 h degrades running and
cycling economy and reduces force production at speed. Plan the two days after
as genuinely easy — a "moderate" run on 48-hour eccentric soreness is a session
that costs more than it returns.

## Interactions

- **With `gym/lower-max-strength`:** overlapping stimulus. Run them in the same
  block only if they're 72 h apart and total lower-body sets stay sensible.
- **With `cycling/endurance`:** the best pairing available. Low mechanical
  strain, and the flush is tolerable even with sore legs.
- **With `running/*`:** the worst pairing. Running loads exactly the tissue that
  is sore, at impact.
- **Interference:** this is a strength/hypertrophy stimulus, and interference
  from concurrent endurance work on those outcomes is not well supported. The
  constraint here is **soreness**, not interference — don't over-protect it from
  aerobic work.

## Stop rules

- Sharp or localised pain in a muscle belly during a lowering rep — end the
  exercise, do not finish the set.
- Loss of control of the eccentric (the athlete drops rather than lowers) —
  the set is over; that rep is where injuries happen.
- Soreness from the previous eccentric session still present — postpone rather
  than train through. Stacking eccentric work on unresolved DOMS is the fastest
  route to a strain.
- Any tendon pain above ~3/10 during the session, or worse the next morning.

## Worked example

> **Context:** cyclist, 6 weeks out from a target event, hamstring strain last
> season, currently 3 rides + 2 gym sessions per week, key session is Saturday's
> long ride, HRV and sleep normal.

> **Prescription — Monday:**
> - Warm-up: 8 min bike, then bodyweight hinge and split-squat progressions
> - A1. Romanian deadlift — 4 × 6 @ 4 s lowering, 2.5 min rest
> - A2. Nordic hamstring curl — 2 × 4, assisted, 2 min rest
> - B. Bulgarian split squat — 3 × 8/side, controlled but not tempo-emphasised
> - Note to athlete: expect peak soreness Tuesday night through Wednesday.

> **Placement rationale:** Monday puts 5 days between this and Saturday's key
> ride, well past the 72 h window. Tuesday becomes Z2 rather than sweetspot.
> Nordics start at 2 × 4 because the hamstring history means the first exposure
> should be deliberately underdosed.

## Evidence notes

**Supported:**
- High-volume Nordic protocols significantly improve eccentric hamstring
  strength, fascicle length and muscle thickness versus low-volume protocols.
- Programmes including the Nordic exercise are associated with reduced hamstring
  injury rates — but see the caveat below.
- Eccentric work produces a distinctly higher DOMS response than concentric or
  isometric work at matched load.

**Contested — state carefully to the athlete:**
- The widely quoted "~50% reduction in hamstring injury risk" from Nordics comes
  from meta-analyses that pooled programmes containing the Nordic alongside
  other exercises. A methodological reappraisal restricting analysis to trials
  isolating the Nordic could not replicate that result and concluded the
  protective effect remains inconclusive, warranting only a conditional
  recommendation. A more recent pooled estimate across 15 trials still favours
  prevention programmes overall, but with a prediction interval crossing the
  null. Prescribe Nordics for the strength and architecture adaptations, which
  are solid; do not promise an injury-risk number.
- **For painful tendons, eccentric-only is the weakest option, not the
  strongest.** A network meta-analysis of patellar tendinopathy ranked eccentric
  training worst for VISA-P outcomes, with heavy slow resistance better for
  long-term knee function and progressive tendon-loading combined with isometric
  work outperforming eccentric training alone. The "eccentrics fix tendons"
  heuristic is out of date — route tendon pain to `gym/lower-isometric` and
  heavy slow resistance instead.

**Convention (defaults, thinly evidenced):**
- The specific 72 h spacing figure. It's a reasonable read of the DOMS
  time-course, not a tested threshold.
- The ≤8 working-sets ceiling in a first block.
