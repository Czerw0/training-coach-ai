# Gym — lower body, maximal strength

> DRAFT — coach to verify. General strength-and-conditioning guidance, not
> individualised medical advice.

## Use when

Building maximal force production in the legs through heavy, controlled
bilateral and unilateral lifting — squat, deadlift, hinge, leg press, step-up —
and **heavy slow resistance (HSR)** for tendon capacity.

This is the default lower-body gym session for an endurance athlete and the one
with the strongest performance rationale. It belongs in a plan when the athlete
wants to:

- Improve economy/efficiency and late-race durability rather than raw aerobic
  ceiling.
- Build the strength base that `gym/lower-power` and `plyometrics/intensive`
  require before they're safe to prescribe.
- Load a tendon progressively without the soreness cost of eccentric work.

## Don't use when

- Inside race week, or in the last 7–10 days before a key event.
- The same day as a power or intensive plyometric session — heavy fatigue kills
  the velocity those sessions exist to train.
- The athlete has no competent squat or hinge pattern. Send them to technique
  work at moderate load first; heavy loading is not the place to learn a
  movement.

## Prescription

| Variant | Sets × reps | Intensity | Rest | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Maximal strength | 3–5 × 3–5 | ~85–92% 1RM, RPE 7–9 | 3–5 min | Force production, neural drive |
| Strength-hypertrophy | 3–4 × 6–8 | ~75–85% 1RM, RPE 7–9 | 2–3 min | Base building, off-season |
| Heavy slow resistance | 3–4 × 6–8 | heavy, 3 s up / 3 s down | 2–3 min | Tendon remodelling, patellar/Achilles |
| Unilateral strength | 3 × 6–8/side | RPE 7–8 | 2 min | Asymmetry, running specificity |

**Session shape:** one primary bilateral lift, one unilateral or posterior-chain
lift, 1–2 accessories. 45–60 minutes total. For an endurance athlete, **2
sessions per week** during a build block is the well-supported dose; one per
week maintains what's been gained.

**Autoregulation:** stop a set when technique degrades or when bar velocity
drops noticeably. If a velocity device is available, a **velocity-loss threshold
of ≤20%** — ideally 10–20% — is the better volume control: low velocity-loss
training produces equal or better strength gains with less volume and
substantially less fatigue than training deep into a set.

## Dosing and progression

- **Double progression:** hold the rep range, add reps weekly across sets; once
  the top of the range is hit on all sets, add load and return to the bottom.
- Add load in the smallest increment available. A 2.5 kg jump held for three
  weeks beats a 10 kg jump held for none.
- **HSR progression:** load first, tempo held constant. The slow tempo is the
  stimulus — do not shorten it to move more weight.
- **Regression path:** reduce load → reduce range (box squat, partial) → move to
  `gym/lower-isometric`.

## Scheduling

**Hard rules:**
- Not the same day as `gym/lower-power` or `plyometrics/intensive`.
- ≥48 h from power or intensive plyometric work in either direction.
- Not in race week.

**Preferences:**
- Early in the week, or on a day with an easy aerobic session rather than a key
  one.
- If it shares a day with endurance work, **lift first, endurance second**, and
  separate by ≥6 h where the athlete's day allows.
- Do not schedule the day before a long run. Cycling the day after is fine.

**What it costs the next 48 h:** moderate `damage` and real neural fatigue.
Legs will feel heavy for a hard run the next morning; they'll feel almost normal
for Z2 riding.

## Interactions

- **The interference worry is overstated here.** Endurance training frequency,
  training status and session timing all failed to modify the interference
  effect for strength and hypertrophy outcomes in recent pooled analysis. This
  session can sit closer to endurance work than tradition suggests. What
  interference genuinely threatens is `gym/lower-power` — protect that one.
- **With `running/*`:** running interferes with lower-body strength adaptation
  more than cycling does. If the athlete is running-primary and strength gains
  have stalled, that ordering is the first thing to examine.
- **Prerequisite for:** `gym/lower-power`, `plyometrics/intensive`. Do not
  prescribe those to an athlete who has no strength base here.

## Stop rules

- Technique breakdown under load — the set ends, and the next set drops weight.
- Sharp joint or tendon pain during a rep, or a tendon reading above ~3/10.
- Bar speed collapsing on a submaximal set is a fatigue signal, not a challenge.
- If the athlete cannot complete the prescribed reps at a load they handled last
  week, treat the session as a readiness signal and cut it short rather than
  grinding.

## Worked example

> **Context:** runner, base phase, 5 runs/week, wants better economy and a
> hamstring that stops complaining on hills. No gym for 3 months.

> **Prescription — Tuesday and Friday, weeks 1–4:**
> - A. Back squat — 3 × 8 @ RPE 7 (deliberately conservative for re-entry)
> - B. Romanian deadlift — 3 × 8 @ RPE 7
> - C. Split squat — 3 × 8/side
> - D. Calf raise (HSR tempo, 3 s / 3 s) — 3 × 8
> - Weeks 5–8: move A and B to 4 × 5 @ RPE 8.

> **Placement rationale:** Tuesday and Friday keep 72 h between sessions and
> avoid the Saturday long run and Wednesday's intervals by ≥24 h. RPE 7 for the
> first block because the goal in month one is tissue tolerance, not a PR — the
> athlete's aerobic fitness is far ahead of their current lifting tolerance.

## Evidence notes

**Supported:**
- Heavy strength training improves cycling performance (time trial, time to
  exhaustion), driven mainly by **cycling efficiency and anaerobic power**, with
  **no change in VO2max**, maximal aerobic power or the maximal metabolic steady
  state. This is the honest framing: it makes the athlete more economical, not
  more aerobic.
- Adding strength work improves running economy and, more recently, **economy
  durability** — efficiency maintained under fatigue rather than only when
  fresh — and substantially increased time to exhaustion at the end of a long
  run in well-trained male runners.
- Adding heavy strength to endurance training improved both cycling and running
  performance measured immediately after prolonged submaximal work.
- Velocity loss ≤20% produces more effective stimuli for power and speed
  outcomes than higher velocity-loss training; 10–20% is the range most often
  recommended for inducing adaptation while limiting neuromuscular fatigue.
- Two sessions per week has been shown to improve cycling performance, with
  gains maintainable on one session per week.

**Convention:**
- The specific 48 h spacing from power work.
- The RPE anchors. Useful and widely used; not derived from a trial.

**Note on certainty:** the cycling meta-analysis reporting these effects rated
its own evidence certainty as low. The direction is reliable; precise magnitudes
are not.
